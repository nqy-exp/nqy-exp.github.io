## Overview:
After experiment, the **parchment paper** showed no changes in shape or structure after baking, the plan to use it as the main substrate for cross-sectional cutting **failed**. Testing then shifted to ***silicone sheets*** because of their high transparency under a microscope and the ability to clearly identify curing agents. Consequently, further tests were conducted on sample preparation and the curing speeds of different agents on silicone sheets. Finally, a method was found that allows for both ***simple sample preparation and clear observation of fiber cross-sections*** under current limitations.

**Experiment Code**: SE01-03<br>
**Environmental Conditions**: 27.4°C  66%RH

**Experimental Record**:

The parchment paper was baked in an oven. To investigate the effect of baking duration, two different baking times were set:
- BB3: 180°C for 3 minutes per side
- BB10: 180°C for 10 minutes per side<br>
*The paper was flipped every five minutes.*

After rolling up the parchment paper, it was placed in the slicer for sample preparation and observation.

### 2026.09.06 | Second Test of Silicone Sheets as Embedding Media
**Experiment Code**: SE01-05<br>
**Sample ID**: T002<br>

**Experimental Record**:

Using 0.4mm silicone sheets as the substrate, a curing agent (such as the nail polish used previously) was applied to the sheet. The two sheets were then closed and wrapped in an outer layer of parchment paper (for support and to reduce friction when placing it into the slicer) to test if effective slicing could be achieved. This experiment primarily tested whether the curing agent remains stable within the silicone sheets and whether the sliced samples can be clearly presented under a microscope.

Subsequently, an experimental embedding and curing process was performed on T002 fibers.

First, a blank test with the 0.4mm silicone sheet was conducted. The result was ideal, consistent with the 0.1mm silicone sheet, featuring clear edges and a good field of view.

After the nail base coat cured, **it separated from the silicone sheet**, meaning the two sheets split apart during slicing. This causes a major problem: it is very easy for the fiber cross-sections to fall off and be lost. In this run, we can see that there are no traces of nail base coat left on the surface of the silicone sheet. This means that if fibers were included in the test, they would also be lost due to the curing agent peeling off, making it impossible to locate the fiber cross-sections in the field of view.

Based on these issues, UV glue was selected for the next test, utilizing its light-curing properties to achieve stronger adhesion.

This procedure is ***theoretically feasible***, but because it requires cutting the silicone sheets, applying the medium, and then precisely placing the fibers, the requirement for operational precision is extremely high.

After multiple attempts, the cured layer of the embedding medium is clearly visible. Even at 40X magnification (total magnification of 500X), the field of view remains relatively clear, allowing observation of the fiber's twisting structure and blurry cross-sections. This proves the feasibility of this method for cross-sectional slicing after embedding and curing.

- The fibers are not all perfectly vertical, so twisted structures are seen in the slice instead of true cross-sections. This is expected to be improved by optimizing the sample preparation method.
- Imaging clarity still needs improvement. Although some structures are visible, the characteristic spindle-shaped (waist-shaped) hollow cross-section of cotton fibers remains difficult to identify under current conditions. Besides switching to a higher-performance curing agent, this may also require optimized sample preparation.

The next step is to focus on ***optimizing the sample preparation method***. Additionally, more curing agents can be tested to find the most suitable one under existing limitations, specifically:<br>
- High clarity under the microscope
- Ability to bond the silicone sheets together
- Minimizing impact on the fiber structure

### 2026.09.10 | Solidification Speed of Different Curing Agents on Silicone Sheets
**Experiment Code**: SE01-06<br>
**Environmental Conditions**: 27.9°C  61%RH<br>

**Experimental Record**:

This experiment is a preliminary validation for optimizing future processes. Considering the improvements in sample preparation, it is necessary to understand *how the morphology of curing agents on silicone sheets changes during the curing process*, which will provide a basis for optimizing subsequent steps.

The objective of this experiment is to determine the state of different curing agents on the silicone sheet (the embedding medium), including whether they peel off after curing and their solidification speeds.

0.1mm thick silicone sheets were selected for this test. Since this thickness cannot meet the compression requirements of the slicer and is not suitable for formal sample preparation, it is used as scrap material for this validation.

The experimental steps are as follows:

Cut the silicone sheet into squares with side lengths of approximately 10cm. On one side, use a marker to divide it into 9 grids (corresponding to 9 curing agents) for easy observation. After peeling off the protective layer on the other side, use alcohol to disinfect the right side of each grid. 

Then, apply the curing agent to both sides of each grid and begin recording the observations.

**Experimental Analysis**:<br>

<figure style="text-align: center; margin: 2em 0;">
  <img src="/images/con-SI.png" alt="Figure 6: States of different curing agents on silicone sheets" style="max-width: 400px; height: auto; border-radius: 4px;">
  <figcaption style="font-size: 0.85em; color: #555; font-style: italic; margin-top: 10px;">
    Figure 6: States of different curing agents on silicone sheets
  </figcaption>
</figure>

The results are summarized in the table below:<br>

| Curing Agent | Droplet State           | Drying Speed               | Notes                                              |
| :----------- | :---------------------- | :------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------- |
| ZB           | Film-forming            | Slightly slow (~3min)      |                                                    |
| ZT           | Film-forming            | Slightly slow              |                                                    |
| ZZ           | Film-forming            | Slightly slow              |                                                    |
| J5           | Beading                 | Slightly fast (~1min)      |                                                    |
| FW           | Pooling after spreading | Slow (>5min)               |                                                    |
| FA           | Pooling after spreading | Extremely slow (>20min)    |                                                    |
| HT           | Beading                 | Extremely slow (>20min)    | Slight solidification after 20min in small amounts |
| HW           | Beading                 | Extremely slow (>20min)    |                                                    |
| UV           | Pooling after spreading | Immediately after UV light | Difficult to peel on alcohol-treated areas         |

The observations show that the initial morphology of the curing agent depends on its own liquid properties. Curing agents with high fluidity eventually form droplets on the silicone sheet, while the higher viscosity nail polish series can maintain a film-forming state until dry and are ultimately peeled off as a film during the peeling test.

However, FW, FA, HT, and HW (the sewing edge sealants and fabric hardeners) are not suitable for creating new curing methods due to their extremely slow drying speeds.

In the peeling test, except for the UV glue cured on the alcohol-treated silicone surface, which showed strong adhesion (difficult to peel), all other curing agents could be easily peeled off after curing.

Through this experiment, it can be confirmed that ***UV glue is currently the most ideal embedding medium***. It features fast curing speed, high hardness (beneficial for slicing), and excellent adhesion on alcohol-treated surfaces.

Next research focuses:<br>
- Ensuring fibers are all in one direction
- How to make UV glue display fibers more clearly

The next experiments should start from these two directions.

### 2026.09.15 | Cross-sectional Slicing using UV Glue
**Experiment Code**: SE01-07<br>
**Sample ID**: T002<br>
**Environmental Conditions**: 25.7°C  47%RH<br>

**Experimental Record**:

Based on the high clarity observed with UV glue as a curing agent in SE01-05, I considered using a **"sandwich" structure** for embedding, consisting of: silicone sheet – UV glue – fiber – UV glue – silicone sheet.

This structure aims to ensure complete embedding and curing of the fibers while improving visibility under a microscope.

Additionally, by preparing large samples first and then **selecting specific areas** for cutting and observation, the strategy aims to reduce interference from longitudinal structures caused by inconsistent fiber orientation during cross-sectional observation.

First, the 0.4mm silicone sheet base was *disinfected with alcohol*, then UV glue was applied and cured under UV light. Subsequently, the combed fibers were placed on top, more UV glue was applied, and a 0.1mm silicone sheet was placed on top to ensure the fibers were fully embedded between the two sheets. Using different thicknesses of silicone sheets allows for precise control over the total sample thickness by compensating for the extra thickness added by the cured UV glue.

After observing the embedded block, I used scissors to cut out regular samples with consistent fiber orientation for slicing. Scissors were used because they apply force toward the center rather than in a single direction, which reduces the risk of separation between the silicone sheet and the UV glue.

Problems occurred when attempting to use the slicer; the prepared sample was too thick to fit, and the silicone sheet showed signs of wear. At this point, the plan seemed to have failed because the thickness created by the UV glue cannot be easily controlled through standard preparation methods. Creating a mold for this specific size and thickness would be very expensive due to high precision requirements.

Attempting to remove the silicone sheet did not solve the problem as the sample still would not fit into the slicer; the UV glue block had uneven thickness, and some areas showed stress whitening caused by compression. 

Consequently, I decided to abandon the slicer and attempt manual cutting. Surprisingly, the manual slicing was very successful, followed by microscopic observation.

**Experimental Analysis**:<br>

<figure style="text-align: center; margin: 2em 0;">
  <img src="/images/T02C-UV-Su1.png" alt="Figure 7: Microscopic image of T002 fibers embedded in UV glue" style="max-width: 600px; height: auto; border-radius: 4px;">
  <figcaption style="font-size: 0.85em; color: #555; font-style: italic; margin-top: 10px;">
    Figure 7: Microscopic image of T002 fibers embedded in UV glue
  </figcaption>
</figure>

The images are very clear, revealing the sliced structure and distinct layering within the UV glue (likely caused by the UV glue–fiber–UV glue arrangement).

Notably, three cracks appeared in the sample instead of the expected single crack. However, since this preparation did not follow the original plan, these multiple cracks might have been formed during repeated attempts and compression, caused by protrusions from liquid droplets, or as a result of the scissor cutting. Since the cracks do not affect current observation accuracy, they are set aside for now.

Additionally, some small bubbles were present, which may cause visual confusion between the fiber cross-section and the bubble during initial observation. In the future, this can be minimized by optimizing the preparation process or by adjusting the microscope aperture.

<figure style="text-align: center; margin: 2em 0;">
  <img src="/images/T02C-UV-Su2.png" alt="Figure 8: T002 fiber cross-section embedded in UV glue" style="max-width: 600px; height: auto; border-radius: 4px;">
  <figcaption style="font-size: 0.85em; color: #555; font-style: italic; margin-top: 10px;">
    Figure 8: T002 fiber cross-section embedded in UV glue
  </figcaption>
</figure>

**Multiple fiber cross-sections** were eventually found in the field of view. T02C-UV-07 shows a very clear cross-section accompanied by an oblique section. By adjusting the light source, image T02C-UV-08 was obtained, which **clearly displays the characteristic** hollow, spindle-shaped (waist-shaped) structure of cotton fibers.

Therefore, it is confirmed that this method is feasible! Although it was not my original plan, I have found a way to observe fiber cross-sections under limited conditions!!

***This experiment successfully validates the feasibility of the "sandwich" embedding method.*** Using silicone sheets as a supporting skeleton and UV glue as an embedding medium creates an embedded block of a certain thickness for slicing. This method effectively solves the difficulty of observing fiber cross-sections under current limited conditions.

Furthermore, since this system relies on UV light penetration for curing, transparent media (such as silicone sheets) must be used; therefore, parchment paper or silicone oil paper is not applicable in this scheme.

**Future Plans**:

A feasible method for slicing and clearly observing fiber cross-sections has been identified.

Due to *technical limitations*, bubble removal can only be managed through manual techniques, such as applying pressure more slowly during compression. Professional methods like vacuum degassing are unavailable due to the lack of a vacuum pump, and heating methods cannot be implemented because they do not meet safety requirements in a home laboratory setting.

Relatively speaking, the presence of bubbles does not significantly hinder clear observation, as their effects can be minimized by adjusting the lighting.

The next priority is to conduct multiple repeat experiments to verify the stability and reproducibility of this sample preparation method. Once consistent, high-quality cross-sectional observation is achieved, the project will officially enter Phase 1: the systematic collection of microscopic images for both the longitudinal and cross-sectional structures of the fibers.

可以看到两个硅胶皮粘在一起，并且中间有非常清晰的固化层！

虽然我可以继续进行其他固化剂的测试，不过鉴于已显现成功的可能性，决定优先进行针对T002纤维的包埋与切片实验。因为在多次尝试之后终于看到了成功的可能。

在多次尝试后，可以看到包埋剂固化剂层非常清晰可见，并且放大到40X（物镜），也就是整体放大500倍之后，视野内还是相对清晰的，并且可以看到棉纤维的转曲结构和模糊的横截面结构，证明该方法的包埋固化后横截面切片的可行性。

但是依旧存在一些问题：<br>
- 纤维方向并不是都是竖直的，所以会在切片内看到转曲结构，并不是横截面。这个预计可以通过优化制样方式来得到改善。
- 成像清晰度仍有待提升。虽然已能观察到部分结构，但棉纤维特征性的纺锤形（腰型）中空横截面在当前条件下依然难以辨识。这除了更换更高性能的固化剂外，可能还需配合优化的制样方式。

下一步是重点***优化制样方法***。还有也可以测试更多的固化剂，来找到选受限条件下最适配的固化剂，也就是：<br>
- 显微镜下清晰度高
- 能将硅胶皮粘合在一起
- 最大程度不影响纤维的结构

###  2026.09.10｜不同固化剂在硅胶皮上的凝固速度
**实验编号**：SE01-06<br>
**环境参数**：27.9°C  61%RH<br>

**实验记录**：

这次的实验主要是为了后续流程优化进行一个前置验证。考虑到制样方式的改进，需要了解*固化剂在硅胶皮上的的形态随固化过程的变化*，从而为后续制样步骤的优化提供依据。

实验的目的是：不同的固化剂在硅胶皮（也就是包埋剂）上是什么状态，包括固化后会不会剥落，固化的速度。

本次选用0.1mm厚的硅胶皮进行测试。由于该厚度的硅胶皮因难以满足切片器压实要求而不适用于正式制样，故将其作为损耗材料用于本次验证。

实验步骤如下：

将硅胶皮裁剪为边长约10cm的正方形，在其中一面用记号笔划分为9个网格（对应9种固化剂），以便于观察；在另一面剥除保护层后，利用酒精对每个网格的右侧区域进行消毒处理。<br>
随后，分别在每个网格的左右两侧涂布固化剂并开始记录观察结果。

**实验分析**：

（图6：不同固化剂在硅胶皮上的状态）见英文版

| ZB  | 成膜    | 略慢（~3min）  |               |
| ZT  | 成膜    | 略慢         |               |
| ZZ  | 成膜    | 略慢         |               |
| J5  | 聚积    | 略快（~1min）  |               |
| FW  | 平摊后聚积 | 慢（>5min）   |               |
| FA  | 平摊后聚积 | 极慢(>20min) |               |
| HT  | 聚积    | 极慢(>20min) | 量少处在20min后略凝固 |
| HW  | 聚积    | 极慢(>20min) |               |
| UV  | 平摊后聚积 | 照紫外光后立即    | 酒精消毒处剥离较困难    |

观察结果表明，固化剂的初始形态取决于其自身的液体性质。本身流动性大的固化剂，在硅胶皮上最终都会聚集成液体滴状，而黏度更大的指甲油系列在硅胶皮上就可以保持成膜状态直到干燥，并且最后在剥开的实验中也是以膜的形态被揭下来。

然而，固化剂FW, FA, HT, HW也就是缝纫所用的锁边液以及织物固化液，由于其干燥速度过慢，并不适合用于创造新的固化方式里。

剥离试验中，除在经过酒精处理的硅胶皮表面使用UV胶固化后表现出较强的粘附力（难以剥离）外，其余固化剂固化后均可被轻易剥离。

通过这次的实验，可以确定的是：***UV胶是目前最理想的包埋介质***。其具有固化速度快、硬化程度高（利于切片）以及在酒精处理表面具有优异粘附力等特性。

- uv胶怎么样能更加清晰展示纤维

接下来的实验应该从这两个方向入手。

###  2026.09.15｜UV胶固化横截面切片
**实验编号**：SE01-07<br>
**样品编号**：T002<br>
**环境参数**：25.7°C  47%RH<br>

**实验记录**：

基于SE01-05实验中UV胶为固化剂的时候在显微镜下非常清晰，所以考虑做**三明治结构**的包埋，也就是“硅胶皮-UV胶-纤维-UV胶-硅胶皮”结构。

该结构旨在实现纤维的全方位包埋固化，并提升显微镜下的辨识度。

此外，通过先制备大尺寸样品、再**选取特定区域**进行剪裁观察的策略，旨在减少因纤维方向不一而产生的纵向结构对横截面观察的干扰。

首先，对0.4mm厚的硅胶皮基底进行*酒精消毒*，滴加UV胶并经紫外光照射使其完全固化。随后，将梳理好的纤维置于其上，再次滴加UV胶，并覆盖一层0.1mm厚的硅胶皮以确保纤维被完整包埋在两层硅胶皮之间。采用不同厚度的硅胶皮是为了补偿UV胶固化后产生的额外厚度，从而实现对整体样品的精确厚度控制。

随后观察包埋块，使用剪刀从包埋块中剪取纤维方向一致且规整的样本以备切片。使用剪刀剪样是因为这样可以减少硅胶皮与UV胶分离的情况，因为剪切时，力是向中心而非单一方向。

在放入切片器的时候出现了问题，因为制得的样品太厚了，完全无法塞入，并且硅胶皮已经出现了磨损。在这一步的时候，觉得这个方案已经失败了，因为确实UV胶形成的厚度是无法通过制样手段来固定的，如果要去做模具，这个大小和厚度的模具如果需要误差控制，则会非常昂贵。

抱着尝试一下的心态将硅胶皮去除，依旧无法塞入切片器，UV胶的包埋块厚薄不均匀，并且由于挤压应力，有一部分UV胶已经出现积压形成的应力白化区域。

所以决定放弃使用切片器，尝试直接手切制样，没想到非常顺利的切片制样，后进行显微镜观察。

**实验分析**：

（图7：UV胶包埋的T002纤维显微镜图）见英文版

图像非常清晰，可以看到切片结构，UV胶内有分层的情况，这应该是UV胶-纤维-UV胶结构形成的分层。

值得注意的是，样本中出现了三道裂缝，而非预期的单条裂缝。但是考虑到这次制样并非是按照一开始的计划进行，可能是在多次尝试，挤压过程中形成的多道裂缝，也有可能是在制样过程中由于滴状液体突起部分造成了多道裂缝，或者也有可能是剪刀切割的时候导致。由于裂缝暂未影响当前的观测精度，故在本次分析中予以搁置。

此外，样本中存在少量气泡，这在初步观察时可能导致纤维横截面与气泡的视觉混淆。未来可通过优化制样流程或通过调节显微镜光圈来尽量减少其干扰。

（图8：UV胶包埋的T002纤维横截面）见英文版

最终在视野内找到**多个纤维横截面**，其中T02C-UV-07展现的横截面非常清晰，并且旁边还伴有一个斜截面。通过调整光源，可以得到T02C-UV-08这张图，可以**清晰看到棉纤维的特征横截面**——腰圆形有中空的结构。

所以现在可以确定，这个方案是可行的！<br>
虽然并非是我刚开始设定的方案，但是找到了可以在受限情况下，观察纤维横截面的方案！！

***本实验成功验证了“三明治”式包埋方案的可行性。*** 即利用硅胶皮作为辅助骨架，以UV胶作为包埋介质，构建具有一定厚度的包埋块进行切片。该方案有效解决了在现有受限条件下观察纤维横截面的难题。

此外，由于该体系依赖紫外光穿透固化，必须使用透明介质（如硅胶皮），因此烘培纸/硅油纸在此方案中并不适用。

**后续计划**：

目前找到了可以切割横截面，并且非常清晰的进行观察的方案。

由于*技术条件限制*，经过考虑，气泡消除只能通过操作手法，比如挤压时较慢来完成。因为一般使用的真空负压法需要真空机，条件限制无法达到。而加热法对于环境的要求也很高，由于是在家庭环境中完成的实验，无法达到安全要求，所以也无法进行。

相对来说，气泡对观测到清晰图像影响并不大，可以通过调整光线来规避一些影响。

接下来的工作重点是开展多组重复实验，旨在验证该制样方案的稳定性与可重复性。一旦能够实现稳定、高质量的横截面观察，项目将正式进入第一阶段——确定材质的纤维横纵结构的显微镜图像收集。
